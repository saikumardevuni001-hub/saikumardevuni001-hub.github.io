Cybersecurity & cloud security learning roadmap

A structured path from first principles to cloud security, for students, career changers and anyone who wants to protect their own accounts and devices.

What this is: a roadmap of topics, practice tasks and trusted free resources. It is not a video course. Practise only on systems you own or have written permission to test. External platforms may offer optional paid features.

Start here

Six core areas

Learn at your own pace. No paid course is required to use this guide.

01

Cybersecurity foundations

Confidentiality, integrity and availability (CIA), assets, threats, vulnerabilities, risk, security controls and defence in depth.

  • How data, devices and accounts can be exposed.
  • Authentication, authorisation and least privilege.
  • Updates, backups, encryption and secure configuration.
  • Outcome: explain one realistic risk and pick a control that reduces it.
02

Networking essentials

How systems communicate and where common security controls fit.

  • IP addresses, ports, TCP/UDP, DNS, HTTP and HTTPS.
  • Routers, switches, firewalls, VPNs and Wi-Fi security.
  • Why segmentation and monitoring matter.
  • Practice: trace a DNS lookup and an HTTPS connection in a lab.
03

Endpoint and account protection

Safe habits for laptops, phones, browsers and online accounts.

  • Unique passwords with a password manager.
  • MFA; prefer passkeys or authenticator apps.
  • Keep operating systems, apps and browsers updated.
  • Practice: review updates, screen lock, encryption and recovery options.
04

Security monitoring and response

How defenders notice suspicious activity and respond calmly.

  • Recognise unusual logins, alerts and unexpected changes.
  • Logs, SIEM, incident triage and escalation.
  • Preserve evidence and report promptly.
  • Practice: write an alert triage note with time, user, device, evidence and decision.
05

Cloud security fundamentals

Shared responsibility and the controls used to protect cloud services.

  • IAM: users, roles and least privilege.
  • Private-by-default storage, access policies and encryption.
  • Security groups, logging, backups and key management.
  • Practice: design a private bucket and a least-privilege role in a sandbox account.
06

Safe hands-on practice

Practise only on systems you own or have explicit permission to test.

  • Build a small virtual lab or use authorised training labs.
  • Document objective, steps, observations and remediation.
  • Never scan or test another person's system without permission.
  • Outcome: publish a redacted lab report with scope, method, evidence and fix.

How to learn effectively, without paying for a course

Use a repeatable cycle: learn one concept, explain it in your own words, practise in an authorised lab, record the evidence, and review what you would improve. Start with networking, Linux/Windows basics and IAM before cloud controls. Never use real accounts, production systems or third-party networks as practice targets.

  • Learn
  • Practise safely
  • Document
  • Review
  • Repeat

Free Cybersecurity & Cloud Security Learning Path

Sixteen modules, each with key lessons, an explanation, a practice task and key terms. Use the buttons, the module numbers, or the arrow keys on the slides.

Module 1 of 16
01

Module 1 — Cybersecurity fundamentals

  • What cybersecurity is; goals: confidentiality, integrity and availability.
  • Assets, threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, impact and risk.
  • Preventive, detective and corrective controls; defence in depth.
  • Policies, acceptable use, data classification and privacy.
  • Threat actors, attack surfaces and basic threat modelling.

What you should understand

Why cybersecurity protects information, systems and services, and how the CIA triad shows up in real examples such as private records, accurate lab results and reliable business systems.

Try this practice task

Classify five everyday assets, list one threat and one vulnerability for each, then suggest a preventive and a detective control.

Key terms

CIA triad · asset · threat · vulnerability · risk · defence in depth

02

Module 2 — Networking foundations

  • OSI and TCP/IP models; Ethernet, MAC and IP addressing.
  • IPv4, subnetting concepts, private/public IPs, gateways and routing.
  • TCP vs UDP, ports, DNS, DHCP, ARP, ICMP, HTTP and HTTPS.
  • Switches, routers, VLANs, NAT, firewalls, proxies and VPNs.
  • TLS certificates, secure Wi-Fi, segmentation and zero trust basics.

What you should understand

How a connection travels from a device through switches, routers, DNS and transport protocols, and how addressing, ports and network boundaries affect security.

Try this practice task

Draw a small office network with a router, switch, firewall and two VLANs. Label the traffic path for one web request.

Key terms

OSI · TCP/IP · IPv4 · subnet · DNS · DHCP · VLAN · NAT · VPN

03

Module 3 — Operating systems & endpoint security

  • Windows and Linux fundamentals: users, groups, permissions and services.
  • Patch management, secure configuration, host firewalls and endpoint protection.
  • Windows Event Logs, Sysmon basics, Linux logs and audit trails.
  • Disk encryption, backups, removable media and device hardening.
  • Virtual machines, snapshots and safe lab design.

What you should understand

How operating-system users, permissions, services, updates, host firewalls and audit logs work, and how Windows and Linux security practices compare.

Try this practice task

Review security settings on your own computer or a permitted virtual machine. Record update status, firewall status and one useful log location.

Key terms

Hardening · patching · permissions · Event Viewer · Sysmon · auditd · backup

04

Module 4 — Identity & access management

  • Identification, authentication, authorisation and accounting.
  • Passwords, password managers, MFA, passkeys and recovery methods.
  • Least privilege, RBAC, ABAC, privileged access and access reviews.
  • Active Directory basics, groups and Group Policy.
  • SSO, federation, SAML, OAuth 2.0, OpenID Connect and identity lifecycle.

What you should understand

The difference between proving identity and granting permissions, and how least privilege, role-based access, MFA, directory services and single sign-on fit together.

Try this practice task

Create a role-to-permission matrix for three job roles. Mark where MFA and an access review should apply.

Key terms

Authentication · authorisation · MFA · RBAC · AD · GPO · SSO · OAuth · OIDC

05

Module 5 — Cryptography & data protection

  • Encoding vs encryption vs hashing; symmetric and asymmetric encryption.
  • Hash functions, salting, digital signatures, certificates and PKI.
  • Data at rest, in transit and in use; TLS and secure key handling.
  • Key rotation, secrets management, classification and retention.
  • Common cryptographic mistakes and safe implementation principles.

What you should understand

How encryption, hashing, signatures and certificates solve different problems, and why keys, secrets and certificate lifecycles must be managed carefully.

Try this practice task

Compare a password hash, an encrypted file and a digitally signed document. State what each provides and what it does not.

Key terms

Symmetric · asymmetric · hash · salt · PKI · TLS · certificate · key rotation

06

Module 6 — Vulnerability management

  • Asset discovery, vulnerability scanning and authenticated assessment.
  • CVEs, CVSS, false positives, prioritisation and remediation tracking.
  • Patch verification, configuration review and exposure management.
  • Responsible disclosure, scope, written authorisation and rules of engagement.
  • Report writing: evidence, impact, severity and practical remediation.

What you should understand

The vulnerability-management lifecycle: discovery, scanning, validation, severity assessment, remediation and verification, and why a finding always needs context.

Try this practice task

Using a permitted lab or sample report, rank three findings by exposure and impact and write one clear remediation recommendation.

Key terms

CVE · CVSS · false positive · authenticated scan · remediation · disclosure

07

Module 7 — Web & application security

  • How browsers, servers, APIs, cookies, sessions and databases work.
  • OWASP Top 10: access control, injection, cryptographic failures, misconfiguration.
  • Input validation, output encoding, secure authentication and session handling.
  • API authentication, rate limiting, secrets and secure error handling.
  • Secure SDLC, code review, dependency checks and security testing.

What you should understand

How browsers, servers, APIs, sessions and databases interact, the common web weaknesses, and the defensive controls that reduce them.

Try this practice task

In an authorised training lab, inspect a sample request and identify where input validation, output encoding or access control is needed.

Key terms

OWASP · injection · XSS · access control · session · API · secure coding

08

Module 8 — Security operations & SIEM

  • Log sources, timestamps, event fields, normalisation and retention.
  • SIEM concepts, alert rules, dashboards, correlation and triage.
  • Indicators of compromise, threat intelligence and detection basics.
  • Incident lifecycle: prepare, identify, contain, eradicate, recover, learn.
  • Evidence preservation, escalation, communication and post-incident review.

What you should understand

How logs, alerts and a SIEM help analysts detect suspicious activity, and a structured triage process: validate, scope, assess impact, document and escalate.

Try this practice task

Review sample authentication logs. Identify the user, source, timestamp and event pattern, then write a short incident note.

Key terms

SIEM · event · alert · IOC · triage · escalation · incident response

09

Module 9 — Threats, malware & defence

  • Malware types: trojans, worms, ransomware, spyware and botnets.
  • Phishing, credential theft, brute-force attempts and denial of service.
  • Endpoint, email, DNS, network and application security controls.
  • MITRE ATT&CK as a framework for describing adversary behaviour.
  • Backups, recovery testing, business continuity and disaster recovery.

What you should understand

How malware, phishing and credential theft lead to compromise, and how endpoint protection, email filtering, DNS security, patching, backups and user awareness reduce risk.

Try this practice task

Review a sample phishing email and identify its warning signs, then write a defence checklist covering endpoint protection, email filtering, patching, MFA and tested backups.

Key terms

Trojan · worm · ransomware · spyware · botnet · phishing · MITRE ATT&CK · EDR · backup · recovery

Module 10 — Cloud computing foundations

How IaaS, PaaS and SaaS differ, what public, private and hybrid mean, and how regions, availability zones and the shared-responsibility model work.

Public cyber awareness

Stay safe: understand hacker tactics

Attackers often exploit urgency, trust, fear and curiosity rather than advanced technology. Learn the warning signs, pause before acting and verify requests through a separate trusted channel.

1. Phishing emails and fake links

Messages imitate banks, couriers, employers or government. Check the full sender address and the real domain. Open the official site or app yourself instead of clicking.

2. Smishing and messaging scams

SMS, WhatsApp and Telegram messages claim a parcel is held or an account will be blocked. Don't use the link or number in the message; verify independently.

3. Vishing and impersonation calls

Callers pose as bank staff, police, tech support or your boss. Never share passwords, PINs, CVVs or OTPs. Hang up and call a number you trust.

4. Social engineering and urgency

"Act now", "keep this secret" and "you'll lose access" are pressure tactics. Slow down and confirm unusual requests with the person through another channel.

5. OTP, MFA fatigue and account takeover

Never share an OTP or approve a sign-in you didn't start. Repeated prompts can be an attack. Deny them, change your password from the official site and report it.

6. Password reuse and credential theft

One breached site exposes every account sharing that password. Use a unique password per service, a reputable password manager, and MFA or passkeys.

7. Malicious attachments and downloads

Unexpected invoices, job offers, APK files, cracked software and macro-enabled documents can carry malware. Install only from official sources.

8. QR-code scams (quishing)

A QR code can hide a fake site or payment destination. Preview the address, check the domain, and don't enter credentials because a QR code asked.

9. Fake websites, apps and support pop-ups

Lookalike domains and alarming pop-ups push you to pay or give remote access. Use official app stores and never allow remote control to an unsolicited caller.

10. Public Wi-Fi and rogue hotspots

Fake hotspots copy real network names. Confirm the name with the venue, avoid banking on unknown networks, and turn off auto-join.

11. SIM-swap

A criminal moves your number to their SIM to intercept calls and SMS. Set a carrier PIN or port-out lock, and contact your carrier at once if service suddenly stops.

12. Deepfake voice and video

AI can imitate relatives, managers or public figures. Verify urgent money requests on a known number, and agree a verification phrase with family or your team.

13. Business email compromise

Attackers impersonate suppliers or managers and ask for new bank details. Confirm changes through a previously verified contact and use dual approval.

14. USB baiting and unknown devices

A found USB drive or unknown charger may be malicious. Don't plug it in; hand it to IT or security.

15. Ransomware and data extortion

Malware encrypts or steals files and demands payment. Keep protected or offline backups, patch systems, and report suspected ransomware immediately.

16. Cloud and data exposure

Public storage, excessive permissions, leaked API keys and open backups expose data. Use least privilege, private-by-default storage, MFA, secrets management and audit logs.

Before you click, pay, share or approve: STOP

  1. Stop. Don't react to urgency, threats or unexpected rewards.
  2. Think. Was this expected? Is it asking for money, credentials, an OTP or remote access?
  3. Verify. Use a trusted number, saved contact or official app, not the details in the message.
  4. Protect and report. Block the sender, keep evidence, and tell your bank, provider or IT/security team.

Your everyday security checklist

Accounts
  • Unique passwords and MFA
  • Review recovery email and phone
  • Remove old sessions and unused apps
Devices
  • Automatic updates and screen lock
  • Device encryption and backups
  • Install software from trusted sources
Payments and data
  • Verify payment changes independently
  • Never share OTP, PIN or recovery codes
  • Keep sensitive files private and backed up

If you clicked a suspicious link or shared information

  • Stop interacting. Don't enter more information or approve prompts.
  • From a trusted device, change the affected password and any reused ones; sign out other sessions.
  • Contact your bank immediately if card, UPI or banking details may be exposed.
  • If you opened a file or installed software, run a security scan and tell your IT/security team.
  • Keep the message, sender details, transaction references and screenshots as evidence. Don't forward malicious links.
  • In India, report cyber financial fraud on the helpline 1930 and at cybercrime.gov.in ↗.

General awareness education only, not legal, financial or incident-response advice. For workplace incidents, follow your organisation's policy and contact its IT/security team.
